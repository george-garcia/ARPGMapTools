# Basin floor, washes and pastoral life: the Danakil/Afar ground between the escarpment and the salt pan

Scope: Act I zones from the south-west pass (Caravan Approach, zone 01) and the boulder fans at the escarpment foot, across the mid-basin gravel flats (old caravan road, Ruined Outpost), the Mineral Washes corridor, and the playa mud margin before the evaporites. The world is Afar/Danakil first; other deserts appear only as labelled analogues.

How to read this file:
- **Cited Findings** are facts taken from the linked source, paraphrased.
- **Inferences** are mine. Most are art-direction reasoning or general desert geomorphology that I did not verify this session against an Afar-specific source. Treat them as working assumptions to check against the photo references.
- **Gaps** list what I could not source.
- All hex values are my approximations from general knowledge of these materials. None was sampled from a photograph.
- Story context: in the vault README (`/home/claude/arpgstorybible/README.md`, "Map decisions"), the zone names are placeholders, the escarpment road is the main road in, and slavers use it to bring captives to the city.

## 1. What is the ground like at walking scale, from the pass and fans through the gravel flats and washes to the playa mud?

### Takeaway
The basin floor is a stony, wind-stripped desert. The sources confirm three things:
- Alluvial cones lie on red-weathered Tertiary bedrock at the foot of the ridges, and wadis cut into them.
- Large areas are stony desert pavement of wind-smoothed stones.
- Soils are lithosols (thin stony soils) over lava and regosols (loose, poorly developed soils) over basin deposits.

The most specific citable image for the fan zones is one Danakil photo record: "alluvial cones over red weathered bedrock, incised by a wadi". The sources gave walking-scale numbers (clast sizes, crack widths) only for general deserts, not for the Afar itself.

### Cited Findings
- **Fans in the Danakil plain:**
  - A 2006 geomorphology photo (Jan Moeyersons, RMCA African Geographic Pictures Collection, image 1684) shows large alluvial cones over red, weathered Tertiary bedrock where the Asa Ali Ridge ends and the Danakil plain begins.
  - A wadi has cut into the cone.
  - Source: [RMCA GeoPic 1684](https://www.africamuseum.be/en/research/collections_libraries/earth_sciences/collections/geopic/geography/1684/picture). Neighbouring records 1683, 1744 and 1746 come from the same Danakil river-deposits series, but I did not open them: [1683](https://www.africamuseum.be/en/research/collections_libraries/earth_sciences/collections/geopic/geography/1683/picture), [1744](https://www.africamuseum.be/en/research/collections_libraries/earth_sciences/collections/geopic/geography/1744/picture), [1746](https://www.africamuseum.be/en/research/collections_libraries/earth_sciences/collections/geopic/geography/1746/picture).
- **Regional geology and soils:**
  - The bedrock is mostly Tertiary lava flows. Soils are mainly lithosols over lava and regosols over basin deposits.
  - Landforms include basaltic lava fields, sandy plains and wadis, with steep escarpments rising west to the highlands.
  - Source: [Wikipedia, Djibouti xeric shrublands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Djibouti_xeric_shrublands).
- **Surface in the Afar:** Paul Salopek, walking the northern Afar, describes open, empty desert scattered with wind-smoothed stones, with no walls, fences or windbreaks. [National Geographic Out of Eden Walk, "Bones Atop Bones" (2013)](https://outofedenwalk.nationalgeographic.org/2013-01-bones-atop-bones-walking-through-a-colossal-graveyard/)
- **Desert pavement in general:**
  - Wind and water remove the fine particles and leave a sheet of rock fragments that armours the ground below.
  - Nearly half of the world's desert surfaces are this stony type.
  - Source: [USGS, Deserts: Eolian processes](https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/deserts/eolian).
- **Desert varnish in general:**
  - Desert varnish is a dark, shiny coating on long-exposed rocks, made of manganese, iron oxides and hydroxides, and clay. These give it its gloss.
  - Ventifacts are rocks cut, grooved, pitted or polished by wind-blown grit.
  - Source: [USGS, Deserts: Eolian processes](https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/deserts/eolian).
- **Sand ripples in general:**
  - Ripples run perpendicular to the wind, and crest spacing roughly equals the hop length of a saltating grain.
  - Coarse grains collect on the crests. In dunes the reverse holds and coarse grains sit in the troughs.
  - Source: [USGS, Deserts: Eolian processes](https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/deserts/eolian).
- **Wadis as trade corridors:** The salt trading post of Berhale (Berahile) sits on a dry riverbed. Before the tarmac road, the old caravan route ran about four days from Lake Asale to Mekele through rocky gullies. [Arab News / AFP, "Modern life intrudes on Ethiopia's ancient salt trade"](https://www.arabnews.com/node/1085506/amp)
- **Seasonal channels used for water:** Afar villages dig "scoop holes" into seasonal riverbeds. [Frontiers in Climate 2021, water security among Afar pastoralists](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/climate/articles/10.3389/fclim.2021.753888/pdf)

### Inferences
These are general desert geomorphology plus art direction, unverified for the Afar specifically.

**(a) Pass and boulder fans (zone 01, escarpment foot)**
- Fans are coarsest and worst sorted at the apex: boulders of 0.5–3 m sit in a cobble-gravel matrix, angular to sub-angular basalt.
- Material fines downslope to cobbles (6–25 cm) and then gravel (2–6 cm) at the fan toe.
- Old fan surfaces carry varnished pavement. Active channels are pale and fresh-looking, so the fan reads as braided pale channels between darker, older "islands".
- Apex boulders show spheroidal weathering, flaking rinds and grus (crumbled rock) at their base.
- The red weathered bedrock in GeoPic 1684 justifies rust-red cut banks where the wadi incises the fan.
- Cut banks are 1–4 m high, layered with gravel and sand beds and with boulders hanging out of the face. The bank toe collects fallen blocks.

**(b) Gravel flats and desert pavement (mid-basin, caravan road)**
- The pavement is a tightly fitted mosaic of 1–6 cm pebbles over a pale, vesicular silt layer 2–10 cm thick.
- Turning a stone shows lighter, reddish undersides. Desert varnish on the upper faces runs from brown to near-black.
- Footpaths, hooves and wheels break the varnish and expose paler fines. That makes an old caravan road readable for decades as a pale, slightly sunken band 3–8 m wide that fades where sheetwash or sand sheets have smoothed it.
- In some places stones have been cleared into lines along the road edge.
- Sand sheets are only 1–30 cm deep, sit in the lee of stones and shrubs, and carry ripples about 5–15 cm apart and 0.5–1 cm high.
- Small sand shadows, a few tens of centimetres long, trail behind every shrub and stone.

**(c) Wadis and Mineral Washes**
- Wadi beds are flat-floored, with sand and fine-gravel bars, cobble lags and scattered boulders.
- Flood debris lines (driftwood, dry grass, dung, plastic, Prosopis pods) catch on shrubs and roots 0.3–1.5 m above the bed.
- Undercut banks show exposed roots. Channels braid on the open flats and fade into sheetflow.
- Iron staining:
  - Hematite (red) and goethite (ochre-yellow) where groundwater seeps or flood water evaporates.
  - Jarosite-type pale yellow near acid sources.
  - White efflorescent crusts (gypsum, halite) on bank tops and wicking up the sides of clasts.
  - Black manganese films on clast surfaces and in dendrite patterns.
- The mineral pool at the corridor's end would show concentric staining bands: white salt rim, then ochre, then rust, then dark wet mud.
- Dallol's colours (yellow, green, rust) are documented for the hydrothermal field itself (see the other research notes). Staining in the Mineral Washes should be a muted, washed-out version of those colours carried downslope, not the full Dallol palette. This keeps the climax for the salt zone.

**(d) Playa mud margin**
- Desiccation polygons 10–60 cm across, with cracks 0.5–3 cm wide.
- Curled mud flakes 0.5–2 cm thick, curling up at the edges.
- A salt bloom forms in the cracks, and a puffy, crunchy, self-rising crust forms where groundwater is shallow.
- Hoof and foot prints are preserved as cast dimples. Wet patches are darker and slick and can trap animals.
- There is a gradient from silty brown mud into grey-white salt crust.

### Gaps
- No Afar-specific measurements were found for fan clast size, sorting, pavement clast size, varnish thickness, ripple spacing or mud-crack polygon size. The numbers above are general-desert values.
- I did not find a published geomorphology paper on the alluvial fans of the western Danakil escarpment. An Addis Ababa University thesis on Danakil geomorphology turned up in search ([etd.aau.edu.et](https://etd.aau.edu.et/bitstreams/4de61c7e-2da8-454e-8944-13e3a4822101/download)) but I did not open it.
- I found no source on iron-stained washes on the basin floor away from Dallol.

## 2. Which plants grow where in the Afar lowlands, and how do they look in drought?

### Takeaway
The Afar lowlands are Acacia-Commiphora thorn scrub and shrub steppe:
- Scattered Acacia (now Vachellia) tortilis, Vachellia nubica and Balanites on sandy plains.
- Senegalia mellifera and Rhigozum somalense on lava.
- Doum palm (Hyphaene thebaica) only in wadis and hollows with permanent groundwater.
- Calotropis procera dominant on grazed land.
- Invasive Prosopis juliflora, up to about 10 m tall, has taken over roughly 20,000 km² of the Afar plains.

The deepest Danakil floor is close to plantless, so vegetation should thin out toward the playa.

### Cited Findings
- **By habitat (Djibouti xeric shrublands ecoregion, which covers the Afar lowlands):**
  - Sandy plains carry scattered trees and shrubs of *Vachellia tortilis*, *Vachellia nubica* and *Balanites aegyptiaca*.
  - Basalt lava fields carry *Senegalia mellifera* and *Rhigozum somalense* as shrubs or small trees.
  - Wadis and depressions with year-round groundwater carry the doum palm, *Hyphaene thebaica*.
  - Overall the ecoregion is dry shrub steppe and grassland.
  - Source: [Wikipedia, Djibouti xeric shrublands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Djibouti_xeric_shrublands).
- **Dominants in Afar grazing lands:**
  - *Calotropis procera* is dominant in both dry-season and wet-season grazing lands, and *Grewia villosa* is dominant in wildlife reserves.
  - The vegetation is described as Acacia-Commiphora woodland, with grasses such as *Aristida somalensis* and the sedge *Cyperus obtusiflorus*.
  - Study sites lay at 659–775 m. Livestock density was 26.14 TLU/ha in dry-season grazing land.
  - Source: [Ecological Processes 2024, s13717-024-00575-9](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13717-024-00575-9).
- **Prosopis juliflora:**
  - A thorny shrub-like tree with drooping branches, up to about 10 m tall and deep-rooted, drawing about 7 L of water per tree per day.
  - Planted in Afar in the 1970s, it now covers about 20,000 km² of the region and has completely overgrown village paths.
  - Camels spread it by eating the pods and passing the seeds.
  - Its thorns injure livestock, and its pods can block cattle mouths and stomachs.
  - Its dense cover draws hyenas, lions, wild cats and foxes close to villages.
  - It is cleared with chainsaws and machetes.
  - Source: [Gulf Times / AFP, "Ethiopia's invasive prosopis tree chokes livelihoods and land"](https://www.gulf-times.com/article/715057/region/ethiopias-invasive-prosopis-tree-chokes-livelihoods-and-land/amp).
- **Plants in Afar material culture:**
  - Acacia stands give shade and grazing, and acacia and shrubs cover the *duka'a* plains.
  - Thorn bushes are cut for corrals.
  - Hut frames are flexible acacia bentwood, and mats are made from palm.
  - Source: [Chocian 2017, TU Wien thesis, "Wherever it rains we go there: Afar nomadic architecture"](https://repositum.tuwien.at/bitstream/20.500.12708/5313/2/Chocian%20Emilia%20-%202017%20-%20Wherever%20it%20rains%20we%20go%20there%20Afar%20nomadic%20architecture...pdf).
- **Climate figures conflict.** The 2024 Ecological Processes paper gives 325–340 mm/yr precipitation in its results but 500–600 mm/yr and a 33–40 °C mean annual temperature in its methods, without reconciling them ([source](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13717-024-00575-9)). The ecoregion page gives 100–200 mm/yr ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Djibouti_xeric_shrublands)). At Dallol itself, rain is extremely rare ([Wikipedia, Dallol ghost town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dallol_(ghost_town))). The 650–775 m rangeland sites are much wetter than a below-sea-level basin floor.

### Inferences
Species forms below are general botanical knowledge, not verified this session.

**Acacia/Vachellia tortilis (umbrella thorn)**
- 3–8 m tall with a flat or umbrella crown. Paired white thorns, some hooked and some straight up to about 8 cm. Coiled pods. Grey-brown bark.
- In drought it is leafless and twiggy. It is the classic silhouette for the gravel flats and fan toes.
- It grows at fan toes and wadi edges, where groundwater is slightly closer to the surface.

**Prosopis juliflora (mesquite)**
- Multi-stemmed, low, drooping, about 2–10 m tall (10 m is sourced), forming impenetrable thickets.
- Feathery bright-green leaves even in drought, so it stands out as the only lush thing. Yellow straight pods 10–25 cm long litter the ground.
- In story terms it suits the deliberate unsettling green of a thriving invader in a dying land. If the world fiction has no colonial introduction, recast it as a fictional invasive species.

**Calotropis procera (Sodom apple)**
- A 1.5–4 m shrub with large waxy grey-green leaves, corky bark and white-purple flowers. Big inflated green fruit 8–12 cm long that splits to release silky seed floss.
- Milky toxic sap means animals avoid it, so it survives on overgrazed and trampled ground near settlements and roads. It is a good marker of human disturbance.

**Salvadora persica (toothbrush tree)**
- A sprawling evergreen shrub, 1–4 m, with drooping branches.
- It tolerates salt, so it can sit closer to the playa than most species.

**Balanites aegyptiaca (desert date)**
- A spiny tree 4–8 m tall with green spines and dull green leaves. Ochre date-like fruit.

**Hyphaene thebaica (doum palm)**
- A branching fan palm (unusual for a palm), 8–15 m, with forked trunks.
- It marks groundwater, so put it only at the mineral pool, wells or springs. It was also the likely mat material (Chocian says only "palm mats").

**Halophytes for the playa edge**
- *Suaeda* (succulent seablite, low cushions under 1 m, turning red-purple).
- *Tamarix* (feathery grey-green shrub, 2–5 m, salt-encrusted foliage).
- Both are typical of salt margins generally. I did not confirm them for the Danakil this session.

**Grasses**
- *Panicum turgidum* tussocks (0.5–1 m, rigid, pale straw) and *Aristida* (sourced) as sparse straw-coloured tufts on sand sheets.
- They are mostly dead or grazed to stubble in the arid present.

**Condition and browse**
- Browse lines on acacias at about 1.8–2.5 m for goats standing on hind legs and about 3 m for camels give flat-bottomed crowns.
- Dead grey snags and fallen limbs are common. Wood cut for hut arches and corrals leaves stumps and lopped limbs near camps.

**Density gradient**
- Wadi edges have the most cover: scattered trees about 10–50 m apart, plus Prosopis thickets.
- Gravel flats have isolated shrubs 50–200 m apart.
- The playa margin has only halophyte patches, and the inner playa is bare.

### Gaps
- No Afar-specific heights, densities or drought-condition data for individual species.
- No confirmation of *Salvadora persica*, *Cadaba*, *Suaeda*, *Tamarix* or *Panicum turgidum* in the Danakil specifically. (They are plausible, and *Salvadora* and *Tamarix* are widespread in the Horn's arid lowlands.)
- No measured browse-line heights.

## 3. Which animals live on the basin floor, and what signs do they leave?

### Takeaway
The sourced fauna is:
- The critically endangered African wild ass, about 600 left in the Danakil of Ethiopia and Eritrea, tied to distance from water.
- Soemmerring's gazelle, dorcas gazelle, beira, gerenuk and beisa oryx.
- Hyenas, wild cats and foxes drawn to Prosopis thickets near villages.
- Endemic geckos.
- Herded camels, goats, sheep and cattle, with donkeys as water carriers.

Signs such as bones, dung and tracks were not documented in what I read. They belong in the Inferences.

### Cited Findings
- **African wild ass:**
  - Critically Endangered, with as few as about 600 individuals in the Danakil Desert of Ethiopia and Eritrea.
  - Distance to water is the key habitat predictor, and in the dry season distance from settlements matters too.
  - Optimal dry-season habitat is only about 207 km².
  - Source: [Frontiers in Conservation Science 2025, seasonal habitat suitability of the African wild ass](https://frontiersin.org/journals/conservation-science/articles/10.3389/fcosc.2025.1551746/full).
  - Conflict: the ecoregion article places the last known Somali wild ass population on the Buri Peninsula, Eritrea ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Djibouti_xeric_shrublands)). The Frontiers survey, run 2017–2020, studied animals in Ethiopia's Afar, so Ethiopian animals exist. Prefer the Frontiers paper.
- **Other wildlife:** Grazers include beira, dorcas gazelle, Soemmerring's gazelle, gerenuk and beisa oryx. Reptiles include the endemic *Hemidactylus arnoldi* and *Tropiocolotes somalicus* geckos. [Wikipedia, Djibouti xeric shrublands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Djibouti_xeric_shrublands)
- **Predators near villages:** Prosopis thickets bring hyenas, lions, wild cats and foxes into villages to attack livestock. [Gulf Times / AFP](https://www.gulf-times.com/article/715057/region/ethiopias-invasive-prosopis-tree-chokes-livelihoods-and-land/amp)
- **Livestock:**
  - One Afar herder interviewed has 10 cows and over 20 goats and sheep, down from 50–100 cattle ([Gulf Times / AFP](https://www.gulf-times.com/article/715057/region/ethiopias-invasive-prosopis-tree-chokes-livelihoods-and-land/amp)).
  - At the salt flats, hundreds of camels rest in the sun while donkeys shelter in their shade and carry water ([Andy Explores, Danakil part 2](https://andyexplores.squarespace.com/blog-1/danakil2)).
  - Donkeys and mules are key for carrying water containers ([Frontiers in Climate 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/climate/articles/10.3389/fclim.2021.753888/pdf)).
- **Further species surveys:** The Mille-Serdo Wildlife Reserve is the Ethiopian protected area for the wild ass and gazelles ([Wikipedia](https://www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Mille-Serdo_Wildlife_Reserve); [BirdLife IBA factsheet](https://datazone.birdlife.org/site/factsheet/mille-sardo-wildlife-reserve-north-iba-ethiopia)). I did not open either.

### Inferences
General knowledge, unverified here.

**Animal looks**
- The wild ass is greyish-fawn with a white belly, an erect black-tipped mane, a dark dorsal stripe and zebra-striped legs, about 1.25 m at the shoulder.
- It shows up best at dawn or dusk near the mineral pool or the wadi seeps.

**Tracks, dung and bones**
- Hoof prints are clearest in playa mud and wadi sand.
- Camel pads are broad, flat ovals about 15–20 cm across. Goat slots are about 4–5 cm.
- Hyena pugmarks are about 9–10 cm long, with four toes and claw marks.
- Dung ranges from goat pellet scatters, to camel pellet piles, to chalky white hyena dung, which is white because of the bone in it.
- Carcasses and bleached bones of livestock lost in drought or flood should sit along the caravan road and wadi banks. Skulls are a strong silhouette.

**Birds**
- Vultures (lappet-faced, white-backed, Egyptian) circle or perch on snags and settle on carcasses.
- Ostriches walk the gravel flats. Their eggs are about 15 cm and their scrapes are about 1–3 m across.
- Brown-necked or fan-tailed ravens appear near camps.

**Small life**
- Termite mounds are more likely on the higher, slightly wetter fans than on the basin floor (uncertain).
- Lizards (agamas on boulders, geckos at night) and scorpions under turned stones.
- Ant trails, darkling beetles and their tracks on sand, and flies around livestock and water.

### Gaps
- I did not source ostrich, striped hyena, jackal or vulture occurrence on the Danakil floor specifically. All are plausible and widely reported for the Afar, but none was cited this session.
- Termite mounds on the Danakil floor are unconfirmed.

## 4. What does Afar pastoral and travel life look like: huts, corrals, wells, graves, caravans, tools?

### Takeaway
The cited record is strong here (Chocian 2017; Salopek; AFP):
- The Afar *daboyta* is a low hemispherical dome, about 3.7–4.4 m across and 2–2.5 m high, built on a lattice of about 50 lashed acacia bentwood arches.
- Women build it, cover it with 5–10 layered palm mats (often with plastic sheeting at the base), and may pile stones up to 1 m around the base.
- Compounds are walled with volcanic stone 1.5–2 m high, and corrals are built of loose lava stone or thorn brush.
- Stone graves (*qabri* clan clusters, *wadal*/*das* warrior plinths) dot the landscape by the tens of thousands.
- Salt caravans carry roughly 7 kg salt blocks in baskets on camels.

### Cited Findings
All from [Chocian 2017, TU Wien thesis](https://repositum.tuwien.at/bitstream/20.500.12708/5313/2/Chocian%20Emilia%20-%202017%20-%20Wherever%20it%20rains%20we%20go%20there%20Afar%20nomadic%20architecture...pdf) unless marked.

**Daboyta (portable round house)**
- *Size:* dry-season houses at Logya and Dubti are about 3.7–4.4 m in diameter and 1.95–2.5 m high. Measured examples were 4.3 × 2.3 m and 4.4 × 2.25 m. Rainy-season houses can be about half that size. In windier Serdo, Elidar and Kori, houses are lower: 2–3 m across and 1.2–1.5 m high.
- *Shape:* mostly hemispherical, with a radius-to-height ratio of about 1:1 to 1:1.15 in the Lower Awash and taller, at 1:1.3–1.5, in Ali Adayto.
- *Frame:* flexible acacia bentwood arches crossing at right angles, both ends set in the ground. A dry-season house used about 50 arches (25 each way, at least 100 sticks).
- *Arches:* the primary arches are bundles of roots or branches, anchored every 25–35 cm and aligned with the prevailing wind. Secondary arches are spaced 3–15 cm apart.
- *Joints and support:* joints are lashed with sisal rope, bark or cloth. Larger houses have a forked centre pole about 6 cm thick.
- *Base:* stones can be piled up to about 1 m around the base.
- *Cover:* several layers of palm mats, about 5–10 for a small dry-season house. Plastic or waterproof foil is often added at the bottom. Removable side mats give ventilation.
- *Entrance:* about 50–60 cm wide and 100–150 cm high, closed by a rolled-down mat.
- *Interior:* a bed covered with an *olloyta* mat (the bed's size sets the house's minimum size), a hearth, water storage, firewood, *halawwa* bundles, and sometimes steel benches or cupboards.

**Other house types**
- *Elongated house:* about 4–5 m wide, 7.5–8 m long and 2–2.5 m high. It has 8–12 longitudinal arches with transverse arches every 50–60 cm and a ridgepole on three forked posts, one of them tied to the bed. It takes about twice the mats, and reed mats in the Middle Awash.
- *Stationary grass hut:* a similar frame thatched with local grass.
- *Ch'qa house:* vertical poles plastered with mud and straw, a corrugated-iron roof and a stone foundation.
- *Sar bet / tukul:* the highland type, adopted after the 1984/85 famine.

**Who builds, and moving**
- Women build the house, gather the materials, make the mats, sisal rope, baskets, bed frame and containers, and move the house.
- Men cut tree stems for roofs and branches for fences, build the corrals, bring grass and move with the herds.
- Houses are dismantled and carried by camel. Often only the rolled mats travel and the wooden frame is left standing, which makes ready-made "ghost hut" skeletons.
- Satellite-camp shelters are smaller to save weight.

**Compounds, corrals and graveyards**
- Volcanic stone fences are about 1.5–2 m high, and animal corrals are built of loose lava stone.
- A *daala* enclosure holds up to 10 *ari* (houses), with separate pens for calves, camels and cattle.
- Temporary camps use long, medium-height thorn-bush corrals large enough for all the dry stock. Men and boys sleep on blankets beside the pens.
- Graveyards lie on a settlement's edge.

**Mobility, food and dowry**
- Some groups move five or six times a year.
- Milk is sealed for travel in smoked grass bowls.
- The bed mat is part of the dowry.

**Graves** ([Salopek, Out of Eden Walk](https://outofedenwalk.nationalgeographic.org/2013-01-bones-atop-bones-walking-through-a-colossal-graveyard/))
- *Clan graves (qabri):* small "villages" of piled stone on hills or flats.
- *Warrior monuments (wadils / das):* plinths roughly 1–2 yards (about 0.9–1.8 m) high, topped with rows of stones counting the enemies the man killed, dozens in some cases.
- *Death markers:* a single large stone marks the spot where someone died.
- *Scale:* tens of thousands of markers, old and new, often at seasonal camp sites. People live, play and camp beside them and use them to navigate.

**Salt caravans**
- Blocks weigh about 7 kg, are cut from the lakebed with hand axes, and were once used as currency.
- About 5,000 blocks a day reach Berhale on a dry riverbed, and trucks take them on from there.
- The old caravan trek through rocky gullies to Mekele took about four days.
- Lakebed temperatures reach 50 °C.
- Source: [Arab News / AFP](https://www.arabnews.com/node/1085506/amp).
- At the pan, men use axes, long wooden levers and adzes, and cut blocks to size by eye. The blocks are strapped together and packed into large baskets slung over the camels' humps.
- Camels are tied nose-to-tail behind a lead handler.
- Shade at the tea stove is a small umbrella.
- Source: [Andy Explores, Danakil part 2](https://andyexplores.squarespace.com/blog-1/danakil2).

**The jile (qolxad) dagger** ([Wikipedia, Jile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jile))
- About 30 to over 50 cm long, with a double-edged, curved, asymmetric leaf-shaped blade that cuts on its concave side.
- Modern blades are often forged from salvaged car or truck leaf springs.
- The wooden (rarely horn) hilt has a pommel ending in three metal teeth of bronze, zinc or silver, with the middle tooth the largest.
- The sheath is leather over wood with an extra-long tip ending in a knob, sometimes with brass plates. It hangs from a waist belt with a round or square buckle.

**Water**
- The Afar use about four sources per household: scoop holes in seasonal riverbeds, hand-dug wells, ponds, rivers and boreholes. The boreholes have solar pumps, which often fail.
- In the dry season only 1% have water within a 30-minute round trip.
- Women and girls fetch domestic water, with donkeys and mules carrying the containers.
- Source: [Frontiers in Climate 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/climate/articles/10.3389/fclim.2021.753888/pdf).

### Inferences
- **Hut silhouette.** A low, lumpy dome, wider than it is tall, in layered tan and grey mats. A ring of piled stones sits at the base, and modern scraps (blue, white or grey tarp, grain sacks, a scrap of patterned cloth) are tied over the mats. This blends the cited plastic sheeting with art direction.
- **Abandoned hut frame.** Because frames are often left behind, a stripped bentwood lattice dome on the gravel flats is a cheap, authentic "people were here" prop. Arch spacing is about 25–35 cm.
- **Corral.** A dry-stone ring 1–2 m high of unmortared, lichen-free dark basalt blocks of about 20–50 cm. On flats with no stone, a thorn-brush ring (dead Acacia or Prosopis branches piled 1–1.5 m high) instead.
- **Grave sizes.**
  - *Qabri:* low oval or rectangular stone heaps about 1.5–2.5 m long and 0.3–0.8 m high, sometimes with upright head and foot stones.
  - *Wadal:* a squarer, faced plinth about 1–2 m high with a row of small stones along the top.
  - Graves cluster on rises and on the caravan road.
  - These sizes are inferred from Salopek's description and general Horn of Africa cairn practice. Verify against photographs.
- **Caravan props.**
  - Wooden pack frames and saddle pads (palm-fibre mats and rope) on camels, and woven baskets holding salt bars.
  - Yellow plastic jerry cans (20 L) and older goatskin water bags. A blackened tea kettle and small glasses.
  - Lead ropes, nose pegs and a camel bell.
  - Clothing: a sarong-style wrap (*sanafil* or *saro*) and a shawl.
  - The jerry cans, skins and clothing are general Horn of Africa knowledge, not sourced here.
- **Story fit.** A slaver caravan would add neck yokes, chains or rope coffles, cage carts and armed escorts. All of that is fiction, but the Afar caravan grammar (nose-to-tail strings, a lead handler, baskets slung over humps) gives it a believable form.
- **Wells.**
  - A scoop hole in a wadi bed is a 0.5–2 m pit with a ring of wet, dark sand and a trampled hoof field around it.
  - A hand-dug well has a low stone or timber lip, with log or carved-wood troughs and plastic basins beside it.
  - Inferred; not described in the sources I read.

### Gaps
- Chocian's grave chapter and floor and door details were beyond what I read (about 330,000 of 565,000 characters remained). The doum-palm origin of the mats, the hut's door orientation and the construction of burial monuments are not confirmed.
- I found no photo-measured sizes of Afar camel saddles, water skins or troughs.
- *Daboyta* is the name cited by Chocian. *Ari/aari* means "house" in general. *Burra*, which also turns up as a name for the hut, I did not confirm.

## 5. What do desert ruins in the Afar look like after decades?

### Takeaway
The best real Afar example is the Dallol ghost town:
- A potash and salt mining settlement of 1917–1960s, about 130 m below sea level.
- Its surviving buildings, still standing in 2004, are built of salt blocks.
- Its 600 mm Decauville railway from the port of Mersa Fatma, opened in 1918, was dismantled after WWII and almost every trace removed.

Old Afar camps leave stone fences, corrals and graves, and abandoned frames. The Ruined Outpost should be built from local basalt and mud, with salt-block patches nearer the pan.

### Cited Findings
- **Dallol ghost town** ([Wikipedia, Dallol (ghost town)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dallol_(ghost_town))):
  - It lies about 130 m below sea level and has an official population of zero.
  - The railway was built in 1917–1918. An Italian company mined about 25,000 t of sylvite in 1925–1929, the US Parsons Company drilled about 10,000 holes at 65 locations by 1965, and attempts to revive the works between 1920 and 1941 failed.
  - Some structures were still standing in 2004, all of them built of salt blocks.
  - Railway: a 600 mm Decauville line from Mersa Fatma in Eritrea ended 28 km from Dallol and opened in April 1918. The British administration dismantled it after WWII and removed nearly all traces.
  - Paved roads reached the area in 2015, but camels and 4x4s remain the main transport.
  - The page's lead caption calls the site an abandoned Italian sulphur mine, but its body describes salt and potash. Treat "sulphur mine" as unreliable.
- **Hamed Ela**, the present-day salt village, is described as a ramshackle collection of huts. [Arab News / AFP](https://www.arabnews.com/node/1085506/amp)
- **Afar buildings in other materials:**
  - A former military camp at Logya is now used as a school.
  - Ch'qa houses are mud and straw over poles, with corrugated-iron roofs and stone foundations.
  - Source: [Chocian 2017](https://repositum.tuwien.at/bitstream/20.500.12708/5313/2/Chocian%20Emilia%20-%202017%20-%20Wherever%20it%20rains%20we%20go%20there%20Afar%20nomadic%20architecture...pdf).
- **More examples worth checking** (not opened): [WebUrbanist, abandoned wonders of modern Africa (Dallol)](https://weburbanist.com/2013/07/24/mansions-to-mines-7-abandoned-wonders-of-modern-africa/3/), [Atlas Obscura, Dallol](https://atlasobscura.com/places/dallol), [Il Risorgimento Eritreo blog on Dallol (Italian)](https://ilrisorgimentoeritreo.blogspot.com/2009/10/dallol.html), [SwissEduc, Dallol salt](https://www.swisseduc.ch/stromboli/perm/erta/dallolsalt-en.html).

### Inferences
**Ruined Outpost build recipe**
- *Walls:* basalt rubble masonry, 50–70 cm thick, set in mud mortar. The mortar has eroded out of the joints so stones stand proud, and the wall tops have collapsed into talus cones of fallen stone at the foot.
- *Sand:* a wind-scoured base on the windward side and sand ramps 0.5–1.5 m high on the lee side.
- *Salt:* a white salt efflorescence band 20–80 cm above the ground where capillary rise and evaporation concentrate it, with "salt rot" (sugary, crumbling stone faces).

**Metal**
- Corrugated-iron roofs are rusted through, peeled back or gone, leaving bent purlins.
- Steel lintels and rails (cue the Decauville railway) are rusted orange-brown with flaking scale.
- Iron rails or bridge girders can be re-used as lintels and fence posts. That re-use is typical, and the scavenged railway also fits the sourced fact that the line was stripped.

**Scavenging and re-use**
- Doors, timber and metal are gone, taken for reuse. In the Afar, timber is scarce and valuable.
- The empty ruin is reused as a goat pen or windbreak: dung floors, a thorn-brush plug in the doorway, soot on interior walls from hearths.
- Graves often appear near abandoned posts.

**Salt-block buildings near the pan (Dallol analogue)**
- Grey-white, rounded, dissolved edges, blocks fused together. A brown dust skin over the white, and pitting from rare rain.

**Mapping to the story**
- Old outpost = old-war/old-empire structure on the caravan road.
- Present = pastoralists and scavengers re-using it.
- Grit applied deliberately: rust, soot, dung, bleached bones.

### Gaps
- I did not find photos or descriptions of specific Italian forts or road posts on the Danakil floor. Italian colonial military posts existed in Eritrea and at the Danakil margins, but I could not cite any by name.
- How the Dallol salt buildings look today (state of collapse) was not documented beyond 2004.

## 6. What are the air, weather and light like?

### Takeaway
The basin floor is among the hottest places on Earth:
- Dallol averaged 34.6 °C a year in 1960–1966, with a 41.2 °C average high and 46.7 °C in the hottest month.
- Its all-time high is 49 °C, and the salt lakebed can reach 50 °C.
- Rain is extremely rare.
- Heat haze, glare, breezy nights carrying sand, and dust devils up to about 1 km tall are the defining atmospherics.

### Cited Findings
- **Dallol temperatures:**
  - Annual mean 34.6 °C (1960–66), the highest recorded for any inhabited place.
  - Average high 41.2 °C, hottest-month mean 46.7 °C, record 49 °C. Rain is extremely rare.
  - Source: [Wikipedia, Dallol (ghost town)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dallol_(ghost_town)).
- **Lakebed heat:** the lakebed reaches 50 °C. [Arab News / AFP](https://www.arabnews.com/node/1085506/amp)
- **A traveller's day:**
  - Temperatures were in the 40s by morning.
  - Heat haze blurred distant figures, and glare demanded sunglasses.
  - A night breeze left sand in hair.
  - Source: [Andy Explores, Danakil part 2](https://andyexplores.squarespace.com/blog-1/danakil2).
- **Dust devils and dust storms in general:**
  - Dust devils form from intense surface heating and can reach about 1 km tall.
  - Dust storms are driven by rain-cooled downdrafts that spread across the ground, and most of their dust is silt-sized.
  - Source: [USGS, Deserts: Eolian processes](https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/deserts/eolian).
- **Rain seasons:** the Afar rangelands have two short rainy seasons, June–September and March–April, with long dry periods between. [Frontiers in Climate 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/climate/articles/10.3389/fclim.2021.753888/pdf)

### Inferences
- **Flash floods.** Floods would come from storms on the escarpment, not from rain on the basin. Inferred from the sourced highland rain seasons and the rare rain at Dallol.
  - A dry, sunny wadi can flood with no local rain.
  - The flood front is brown, debris-laden and 0.3–1.5 m deep on the fans, and leaves mud drapes, debris lines and fresh pale gravel.
  - That is a strong gameplay hazard and VFX beat for the Caravan Approach and the Mineral Washes.
- **Light.**
  - Midday is flat and white, with near-zero shadow under a high sun and a bleached sky (pale grey-blue to white near the horizon).
  - Golden hour is short and intense, with the escarpment glowing rust and ochre.
  - Mirage "water" lies on the gravel flats and playa: inferior mirages that reflect the sky as silver bands, with a shimmer distortion layer near the ground.
- **Night.** Still hot, probably 25–35 °C, with very clear stars and a dark escarpment silhouette. Lamp and fire glow inside the huts.
- **Haboob.** A brown-orange wall of dust driven by an outflow from a storm over the escarpment. It is plausible but I did not document it for the Danakil.

### Gaps
- No sourced figures for night lows, wind speeds, dust-storm frequency or flash-flood frequency on the Danakil floor.
- No sourced documentation of haboobs or mirages in the Danakil specifically.
