# Acid Field and Sulfur Workings: Dallol hydrothermal field, adjacent features, the Dallol mining camp, and real sulfur mining (Kawah Ijen analogue)

Scope: visual basis for the Act I zone "Acid Pits" (acid field on a low dome at the salt pan's southern edge, acid-green pools, sulfur-yellow pools with orange rims, amber sulfur-crust ground, a "sulfur workings" site where captives cut sulfur for the slaver city).
Dallol and the Danakil come first. Everything about Kawah Ijen (Java) is marked **[ANALOGUE: IJEN]**: it is a high, wet, cool volcano crater, not a hot salt dome, so only its mining practice transfers, not its landscape.

Conventions: "Cited Findings" are sourced facts (paraphrased). "Inferences" are my reasoning or art-direction suggestions; they are not sourced and must not be presented as fact. Hex values are all inference, estimated from the colour words in sources and the general look of published photography, never measured.

Note on sources: two primary papers could not be fetched (Kotopoulou et al. 2019 in ACS Earth and Space Chemistry, PMC6380227, blocked by bot checks). Their content reaches these notes through the 2019 Frontiers in Earth Science Dallol paper and the Wikipedia summary that cites Kotopoulou. Anything that depends only on Kotopoulou is flagged.

## 1. Dallol's hydrothermal features at walking scale: springs, chimneys, pillars, terraces, crusts, eggs, films, sizes, formation speed, colour sequence and chemistry, pH, temperature, gases, sound, steam

### Takeaway
Dallol is a 40-60 m high salt dome, about 3 x 1.5 km, about 120 m below sea level. On it, hyperacid (pH at or below 0), hypersaline, anoxic, iron-rich brines up to about 108-110 C build salt cones, chimneys and pillars from centimetres up to about 2 m high, plus terraced pools. They are white when fresh and green-yellow from reduced iron, then oxidise through yellow, gold and orange to red, ochre and brown as they die. Springs switch on and off within days. The colours come from inorganic iron chemistry, not from life.

### Cited Findings
**Dome scale and setting**
- The dome is a "complex 40 m-high" structure on a salt plain about 120 m below sea level. Its central crater is about 1 km across and about 15 m deep. Rainfall is under 200 mm/yr and the mean annual temperature is about 34.5 C — [Frontiers in Earth Science 2019, Dallol dome paper](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feart.2019.00351)
- Other sources give the height as about 60 m above the plains, over an area of about 3 x 1.5 km, at about 125 m below sea level, with a probable collapsed crater near the centre — [Wikipedia, Dallol (hydrothermal system), citing Roscoe/PhotoVolcanica and GVP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dallol_(hydrothermal_system)); Mount Dallol is also called "a mound about 60 m high" of evaporite and hydrothermal deposits — [Applied Volcanology 2015, Gaet'ale paper](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13617-015-0042-x). **Conflict:** 40 m (Frontiers 2019) vs about 60 m (others), probably because they measure from different bases.

**Temperature, pH, salinity, oxygen**
- The brines show pH at or below 0, salt contents above 30% and temperatures up to about 110 C. Spouters emit brine at about 108 C. One degassing hot spot was estimated at about 200 C — [Frontiers 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feart.2019.00351)
- The brines carry more than 26 g/L iron, are nearly ten times saltier than seawater, and are anoxic (citing Kotopoulou et al. 2018/2019) — [Wikipedia, Dallol (hydrothermal system)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dallol_(hydrothermal_system))
- Some pools have negative pH. The yellow-greenish colour of the hyperacid pools comes from reduced iron — [EurekAlert release on Belilla et al. 2019, Nature Ecology & Evolution](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/708841)
- In the 1920s, hot springs near Black Mountain and Mount Dallol reached about 130 C — [Frontiers in Earth Science 2023, Yellow Lake Fissure paper](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feart.2023.1250702/full)

**Forms and sizes**
- Salt cones are centimetre to decimetre scale. Pillars and chimneys reach up to about 2 m high — [Frontiers 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feart.2019.00351)
- Small, widespread, temporary geysers build salt cones. The mineral forms include salt pillars, miniature geysers, "water-lily" and flower-like crystals, egg-shaped crusts and pearl-like spheres (citing Kotopoulou et al. 2018) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dallol_(hydrothermal_system))
- Travel and press descriptions of pillar shapes include "artichoke-like", "mushroom-like" and "barnacle-like". The crust is described as "eggshell-thin" over acid water — [ExplorersWeb, "Dallol: Hell on Earth"](https://explorersweb.com/dallol-hell-on-earth/) (popular source, used for shape vocabulary only)
- The acidic terraced pools show green, orange and yellow tones from iron, manganese and sulfur at different oxidation states. Active salt deposits are white, yellow and brown, and the mounds are often white to yellow — [Frontiers 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feart.2019.00351)
- "Black domes" are dense, dark halite rich in hematite (Fe2O3), with hexagonal joints that suggest cooling and contraction — [Frontiers 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feart.2019.00351)
- Native sulfur occurs as yellow deposits around degassing vents and in small open pits — [Frontiers 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feart.2019.00351)
- A "Skating Rink" is a circular structure about 300 m across — [Frontiers 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feart.2019.00351)

**Colour sequence and chemistry**
- Over time the colours move from white through green, lime, yellow, gold, orange and red to purple and ochre. This comes from inorganic oxidation of iron phases, not from microbes (unlike Yellowstone). Old, inactive springs turn dark brown. The minerals include halite, jarosite, hematite, akaganeite, other Fe-oxyhydroxides, gypsum, anhydrite, sylvite and carnallite — [Wikipedia, citing Kotopoulou et al. and Roscoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dallol_(hydrothermal_system))

**Formation speed and lifetime**
- Springs switch between active and inactive, and new ones appear elsewhere, often within days (Kotopoulou) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dallol_(hydrothermal_system))
- Centimetre-scale changes in pond level show within a few days. The active geothermal area was 0.087 km2 (2012), 0.038 km2 (January 2016) and 0.144 km2 (January 2017) — [Frontiers 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feart.2019.00351)
- The dome probably formed less than about 6,000 years ago — [Frontiers 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feart.2019.00351)

**Gases, steam, sound**
- The main gases are CO2, H2S, N2 and SO2, with traces of H2, Ar and O2 (Darrah et al. 2013) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dallol_(hydrothermal_system))
- Fumaroles and steaming ground occur. In January 2019 a vapour and gas column stood over the dome — [Frontiers 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feart.2019.00351)
- At the nearby Yellow Lake Fissure, "bubbling sounds can be heard from the chimneys" — [Frontiers 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feart.2023.1250702/full)
- Phreatic (steam) eruptions: 1926, forming a crater of about 30 m inside the dome (Lee & Siebert 2010) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dallol_(hydrothermal_system)); a phreatic eruption in 1929 and an ash cloud in January 2011 — [Applied Volcanology 2015](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13617-015-0042-x); see also [Smithsonian GVP, Dallol](https://volcano.si.edu/volcano.cfm?vn=221041). **Conflict:** 1926 vs 1929.

### Inferences
- **Colour as a clock.** Colour reads as age: white or acid-green means live and dangerous now; yellow and gold mean days old; orange, rust and red mean dying; brown and purple-ochre mean dead crust. Set it up so the player learns this, then use it for hazard telegraphing: the greener the pool, the more deadly. This matches the map spec (acid-green pools, yellow pools with orange rims).
- **Walking-scale kit sizes** (from the sizes above): micro-cones 5-30 cm; chimneys and pillars 0.5-2 m (2 m is the cap for realism, so taller ones are stylisation); terrace pools 0.3-3 m across with rims 1-10 cm high; pearl or egg spheres 1-5 cm (estimated; no size found); circular structures up to about 300 m as landmarks.
- **Smell** (unsourced for Dallol itself, but follows from the H2S and SO2 above): rotten-egg H2S plus sharp, acrid SO2 and a chlorine-like acid tang. In VFX, show it as low, heavy haze in hollows.
- **Sound:** hiss of spouters, bubbling and popping of brine, the crunch and crack of thin crust underfoot.
- **Oily films:** the Frontiers 2023 note of an oil-film look caused by dissolved salts (see section 3) supports a high-gloss, iridescent-sheen pool shader.

### Gaps
- No measured sizes found for the "eggs" and "pearl" spheres, the terrace steps or the pool depths on the dome itself. The Kotopoulou paper (which has them) could not be fetched.
- No precise hour-by-hour time for green-to-orange oxidation was found. Only "within days" for spring switching.
- No measured gas concentrations, and no first-hand description of smell or of steam at different times of day.

## 2. The Dallol salt mountain/plateau, salt canyons, pillars and layered walls: shapes, heights, colours

### Takeaway
The western and southwestern slopes of the dome are cut by water into deep salt canyons, caves and sinkholes. Hard anhydrite caps protect the softer halite beneath, leaving isolated buttes, pinnacles and "demoiselles" (hoodoos). This is the natural home of mushroom-shaped salt towers. There is also a Black Mountain of ejected salt boulders and dark, iron-rich salt.

### Cited Findings
- The western dome shows "deep salt canyons", caves and sinkholes formed by dissolution (karst) and drainage along fractures. Less-soluble anhydrite caps protect the halite below, producing isolated buttes, pinnacles and demoiselles. Brecciated, collapsed anhydrite-gypsum lies near dark sinkholes — [Frontiers 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feart.2019.00351)
- The southwestern slopes have water-eroded salt canyons, pillars and blocks — [Wikipedia, citing Roscoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dallol_(hydrothermal_system))
- The Black Mountain lies about 2 km SW of the crater. It carries big salt boulders thrown out of geothermally active fissures (recent explosive activity) and frequent massive bischofite (MgCl2) flows from vents near its SE edge. The Round Mountain is about 3-3.5 km W — [Frontiers 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feart.2019.00351)
- The evaporite sequence under Lake Asale is about 2 km thick — [Applied Volcanology 2015](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13617-015-0042-x)
- Halophilic archaea live in the saline canyons, though not in the acid pools — [EurekAlert / Belilla et al. 2019](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/708841)
- Photo references: [travel2unlimited, Dallol Salt Canyons](https://travel2unlimited.com/?p=288043); [Colored Splendor, "Salt Mushrooms"](https://coloredsplendor.myportfolio.com/salt-mushrooms); [Colored Splendor, "Mysterious salt-rock zone"](https://coloredsplendor.myportfolio.com/mysterious-salt-rock-zone) (photo portfolios, not fetched/verified for captions)

### Inferences
- **Hoodoo kit:** demoiselles 2-10 m tall (estimate; no heights found), each with a pale, harder anhydrite cap (cream to grey) over a waist eroded by rain (pinkish-white to buff halite, banded). Canyon walls show alternating salt and clay or anhydrite bands, so build a tiling layered-wall material with white, buff and red-brown stripes. Band heights and colours are not measured: art-direct them.
- **Skyline at km scale:** a low, pale, lumpy dome only 40-60 m above a flat white pan. Put a steam or vapour column over it as the landmark (sourced for January 2019), and the dark Black Mountain as a counterweight, with the red-ochre escarpment far beyond.
- The story places the acid field on a low dome at the salt pan's southern edge. Real Dallol sits in the north of the Danakil salt plain, so this is a creative relocation. Its form (a low dome above a pan, with canyons on one flank) is consistent with the real feature.

### Gaps
- No measured heights for the salt canyons, pillars or demoiselles were found. No measured colour or band data for the layered walls.

## 3. Black Lake, Yellow Lake, Gaet'ale: looks, dead birds and insects, danger signs

### Takeaway
These are the killer pools. Gaet'ale is a 70 m, orange-yellow, CO2-belching acid lake ringed with brine-pickled dead birds and insects. The Black Lake is a 15 m, 70 C, pH 3, oily-black magnesium-chloride brine with quicksand-like hot mud edges. The Yellow Lake and Yellow Lake Fissure are lukewarm, bubbling, pH 3.4-3.7 pools with red sulfur patches and oil-sheen surfaces. None of them holds any life.

### Cited Findings
**Gaet'ale ("Yellow Lake" in some tourist usage)**
- A roughly circular lake about 70 m across, about 1.5 m below the salt flat, about 3.8 km SE of the Dallol crater. pH about 3.5-4. Yellowish-orange to reddish-orange from dissolved iron salts, with bright yellow spring jets. In 2012 it was opaque, muddy orange. It feels greasy or soapy because it is supersaturated with salts, not because of oil. No sulfur smell. The gas is almost certainly CO2 — [Applied Volcanology 2015](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13617-015-0042-x)
- At least ten birds were found dead around it and well preserved by the brine (the largest a black-headed heron), along with dead insects (a glider beetle, a locust, moths). CO2 pools on the lower terraces, where most of the birds lay. The salt crust may not bear a person's weight. The edge is a scalloped halite ledge with an undercut fringe, with rain-washed debris on the margins. The spring bubbles in several small fountains, and locals report a geyser up to 3 m high for about three years after a January 2005 earthquake (but 2003 satellite imagery already shows the lake) — [Applied Volcanology 2015](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13617-015-0042-x)
- The paper reports total dissolved solids of "96 to 110.4 ppm" (as relayed by the fetch). That is implausibly low for a hypersaline brine, so it is probably g/L or a units slip. **Treat it as unreliable.**

**Black Lake / "Black Water"**
- About 15 m across and about 10 m deep, pH about 3, about 70 C, "extremely hypersaline (>70%)", dominated by bischofite (MgCl2). Its appearance is dated to a 1926 phreatic explosion — [Frontiers 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feart.2019.00351)
- About 70 C and strongly acidic. Being more open, it builds up less CO2, but deadly vapours have occasionally been seen, and two dead birds were reported in January 2011. Its edges are hot, quicksand-like mud. A scientist was badly burned after falling into such mud in the early 1970s — [Applied Volcanology 2015](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13617-015-0042-x)

**Yellow Lake and Yellow Lake Fissure (YLF)**
- The Yellow Lake is a bubbling brine pool at about 40 C, 1-2 m below the salt plain, about 4 km SE of the dome, with halite, carnallite and other metal-salt deposits around its margin. Smaller bubbling ponds line up N-S along a rift fault — [Frontiers 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feart.2019.00351)
- The YLF is a 4.5 km fissure that opened in October-November 2004. Its pools are mostly under 1 m long, a few reach several metres, and a 1.5 m rod found no bottom in the largest. Measured in 2018: Yellow Pool 32.0 C / pH 3.7, Red Pool 35.1 C / pH 3.5, Yellow Lake 41.2 C / pH 3.4, all close to air temperature. Other features: polygonal salt plates several cm thick, some tilted into tepees; 5-10 cm ridges along the plate edges; red, soft, sticky salt-mud patches; red salt crust up to about 50 cm above the flat; red circular sulfur patches 1-2 m across at regular spacing; salt-encrusted bubbles up to a few cm; holes with haloes of halite crystals; one pool with an oil-film look from the dissolved salts; native sulfur crystals; and dead birds sometimes seen at the pool margins, put down to CO2 — [Frontiers 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feart.2023.1250702/full)

**Life**
- No life was found in the Black or Yellow lakes (Mg-rich brines) or in the hyperacid ponds — [EurekAlert / Belilla et al. 2019](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/708841)

### Inferences
- **Danger-sign grammar for gameplay:** a ring of perfectly preserved dead birds and insects marks invisible CO2 (no smell, no visible gas), so make it a hazard zone with a low, faint heat-shimmer or distortion layer in lower terraces. Undercut, scalloped white ledges mean the edge will collapse. Black, glossy, steaming water with mud margins means scald and sink.
- Gaet'ale is the strongest "sulfur-yellow pool with orange rims" reference: yellow jets in orange water inside a white scalloped rim.

### Gaps
- No reliable photo-caption-level description of the Black Lake surface colour beyond "black". The name and the hematite and bischofite chemistry suggest a dark, glossy surface, but that was not described in fetched sources.

## 4. The Dallol potash mining camp ruins (Italian, 1910s-1920s, and later)

### Takeaway
Dallol "town" is a ghost camp of buildings made of salt blocks, served in 1918 by a 600 mm Decauville light railway from Mersa Fatma (Eritrea) that stopped about 28 km short of Dallol. The railway was later dismantled entirely. The salt-block walls still stand in part, sagging and leaning outward as they dissolve.

### Cited Findings
- A 600 mm Decauville railway was built 1917-18 from Mersa Fatma to a terminal (called "Iron Point") about 28 km from Dallol, via Kululli. About 51,000 t of potash is reported for 1918. Production stopped after WWI. An Italian company mined about 25,000 t of sylvite (about 70% KCl) in 1925-29. After WWII the British administration dismantled the railway and removed all traces. Parsons (US) drilled about 10,000 holes at 65 sites by 1965. As of 2004 some buildings still stood, all of salt blocks. Camel caravans still pass for salt. Paved roads arrived in 2015 — [Wikipedia, Dallol (ghost town)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dallol_(ghost_town))
- Many buildings in the Italian mining camp were made from salt blocks. Left to decay, the walls now lean outward before they eventually collapse — [SwissEduc / Stromboli Online, Dallol salt page](https://swisseduc.ch/stromboli/perm/erta/dallolsalt-en.html?id=6)
- Sulfur was mined near the Dallol mining village from 1949 to 1954 by the Dallol Potash, Magnesium and Sulfur Mines company, then by Ralph M. Parsons Co. from 1954 to 1968 — [Frontiers 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feart.2019.00351). **Conflict:** Wikipedia describes Parsons's 1960s work as surveys and drilling, not production.
- Climate at the camp (1960-66 records): annual mean 34.6 C, mean daily maximum 41.2 C, June mean maximum 46.7 C, record 49 C (Pedgley 1967) — [Wikipedia, Dallol (ghost town)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dallol_(ghost_town))

### Inferences
- **This history directly supports the story's "sulfur workings"**: real sulfur was mined at Dallol in the mid-20th century, so captives cutting sulfur there is grounded.
- **Ruin kit:** salt-block walls (blocks roughly 30-60 cm, an estimate) with rounded, rain-melted arrises, tilting outward with bulging bases. The tops dissolve into lumps, and a rust-coloured bloom grows where iron fittings were. Add Decauville-gauge track fragments and tip wagons as props. The real rails were removed, so in-world rails are creative licence or survivors.

### Gaps
- No sourced inventory of surviving machinery, rails, roofing or interiors at the camp. No measured block sizes or wall heights. Recent condition (after 2004) is not documented in fetched sources.

## 5. Sulfur mining analogue [ANALOGUE: IJEN]: pipes, solidified sulfur, tools, baskets, loads, gas protection, blue flames, paths, huts, debris

### Takeaway
At Kawah Ijen since 1968, fumarole gas is piped through ceramic pipes. Molten sulfur oozes out deep red, cools to bright mustard-yellow mats and slabs, and miners break it with metal bars into chunks. They carry 68-100 kg in a pair of baskets on a shoulder pole out of a gassy crater, in rubber boots or flip-flops with wet cloths over their faces, for a few cents per kilo. At night burning sulfur gas makes blue flames up to 5 m high. This is close to an exact template for captives cutting sulfur.

### Cited Findings
- Ceramic pipes are set on an active vent by the crater lake. The gas condenses into liquid sulfur, which drips onto hard sulfur mats and solidifies. Miners break the slabs with a metal pole and pack them out. Reed baskets weigh 150-200 lb (about 68-91 kg). The route is about 2 miles up the mountain, then more than 900 yards down into the crater. There are about 300 miners and about 14 t/day. Few have gas masks; most use wet scarves or rags. The gas makes eyes water, burns throats and lungs and can dissolve teeth. Deformed spines and bent legs are common. Life expectancy is barely over 50 (photographer's claim). Photographer: Luca Catalano Gonzaga — [National Geographic, "The Struggle and Strain of Mining Devil's Gold" (2015)](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/photography/article/the-struggle-and-strain-of-mining-devils-gold)
- Molten sulfur is deep red and turns bright yellow on cooling. It flows slowly from the pipe ends, pools on the ground and is broken into large pieces. Loads are 75-90 kg; the climb is 300 m to the rim at a 45-60 degree gradient, then 3 km down to the weighing point; most miners make two trips a day; about 200 miners; about 14 t/day (about 20% of the daily deposit); about US$13/day (2010). Blue fire is burning sulfur gas, up to 600 C, with flames up to 5 m high, the largest blue-flame area in the world. The crater lake is turquoise, about 1 km wide, 200 m deep, pH below 0.3 (2001) and 0.13-0.5 (2008) — [Wikipedia, Ijen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ijen)
- Sulfur "oozes out of the pipes red-hot and molten" and hardens golden-yellow. Miners strike off chunks with quick blows and fill a large pair of baskets balanced on the shoulders, with mustard-yellow chunks heaped up. A round trip from lake to rim takes about an hour. Grey-white plumes engulf workers in sudden gusts. The stench of H2S and SO2 stings the eyes. The path is steep, winding and narrowing, over loose stone and ledges beside a sheer drop, to an ochre, barren rim. At the weigh station below the rim loads go on a scale, paid at about 7 US cents/kg, and a truck takes them to a factory where the sulfur is melted and spread into sheets. Miners wear cheap rubber boots or flip-flops, homespun scarves over their faces, often bare chests, and have a reddish-brown callus on the shoulder where the load rests — [Hidden Compass, "Out of the Smoky Abyss" (2020)](https://hiddencompass.net/story/out-of-the-smoky-abyss/)
- Miners place ceramic pipes on the vents and spray water onto them. A goat-head offering is buried in the pipes in an annual mid-December ritual begun in 1976 after four miners died. There is a cabin near the crater where miners pray. About 100 kg/day per miner, about $6/day, about 7 cents/kg. Some wear full-face gas masks. The lake's acidity is likened to car-battery acid — [Al Jazeera, "In Pictures: Mining sulphur in Indonesia" (2014)](https://www.aljazeera.com/gallery/2014/1/14/in-pictures-mining-sulphur-in-indonesia)
- **Conflicts:** loads of 68-91 kg (NatGeo), 75-90 kg (Wikipedia), about 100 kg/day (Al Jazeera); route geometry differs between sources; wages vary by year and source.

### Inferences
- **Translation to the Acid Pits:** the slaver regime would condense sulfur at vents in clay or salt-mortar pipes (a ceramic-pipe rack fanning down a slope). Under the pipe mouths are red-black molten puddles crusting yellow, with drip "icicles" and cascading flowstone in yellow and orange (expected from dripping melt, but not sourced; see Gaps). Captives lever slabs with iron bars, break them into chunks and load paired baskets on a carrying pole. A weigh station with a beam scale, tally boards and an overseer platform sits at the field's edge, with a pallet yard of stacked yellow slabs and a smelting hearth that remelts and pours sheets.
- **Night:** blue sulfur flames (up to 5 m) are the zone's night beauty moment. Dallol does not have Ijen's blue fire, so this is licensed analogue colour: acid-green and yellow by day, electric blue at night.

### Gaps
- Sulfur stalactites at pipe mouths, carved sulfur souvenirs, bamboo versus wooden shoulder poles, and exact basket dimensions were not confirmed in fetched sources.
- No source found for Dallol's own 1950s sulfur-mining methods (open pits? retorts?). Frontiers 2019 only notes "small open pits" with native sulfur.

## 6. Life, air, weather and light at Dallol

### Takeaway
There is no life in the pools. Halophilic archaea live only in the surrounding salt plains and canyons. Animals that come near die and are pickled. The air is about 35 C on average, up to about 49-50 C, and does not cool off at night, with CO2, H2S and SO2 venting and rising steam columns. Plants appear only far off the pan (not sourced here).

### Cited Findings
- No life in the hot hyperacid hypersaline ponds or in the Black and Yellow lakes. Magnesium salts are a key barrier. Hyperdiverse halophilic archaea live in the surrounding desert and saline canyons. Winter daytime temperatures can exceed 45 C — [EurekAlert / Belilla et al. 2019](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/708841); [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2019-11-scientists-earth-life.amp)
- Ultra-small structures reported as Nanohaloarchaea in mineral deposits (Gómez et al. 2019). A follow-up warns that rounded mineral nanoprecipitates can mimic cells — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dallol_(hydrothermal_system))
- Dead animals and insects litter the ground near the pools. The heat "does not cool off at night." Temperatures reach about 50 C and average about 35 C — [ExplorersWeb](https://explorersweb.com/dallol-hell-on-earth/) (popular source)
- Mean annual temperature 34.6 C, record 49 C — [Wikipedia, Dallol (ghost town)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dallol_(ghost_town))

### Inferences
- **Light:** white salt under a near-vertical sun gives extreme glare. Grade the zone with high exposure and bleached shadows by day, an amber-pink low sun at dawn and dusk, and heat shimmer over the pools. At night, a warm sky with the faint glow of steam lit by torches or blue flame.
- **Vegetation:** none on the dome or pan. Any plant life (likely acacia scrub or doum palms, not sourced) belongs only at the escarpment foot and in wadis. Never put plants in the acid field.
- **Wildlife at the margins:** dead birds and insects (sourced). Live ones at the edges are unsourced; ravens or kites over the camp would be a creative choice.

### Gaps
- Nearest vegetation and live wildlife around Dallol were not researched in fetched sources.
- No source on haze or dust, wind patterns, or night sky conditions at Dallol.

## 7. Zone sheet for environment art: palette, asset seeds, beauty and grit, reference URLs

### Takeaway
Build the Acid Pits from a white-salt base with live colour pools (acid-green, lime, sulfur-yellow) whose rims age to gold, orange, rust and brown. Add black hematite-salt domes, mushroom hoodoos and a sulfur-workings yard of ceramic condensing pipes, yellow slabs, iron bars, baskets and a weigh station. It should be the loudest-coloured place in the act.

### Cited Findings (basis for the palette and seeds; all detailed above)
- Colour sequence from white through green, lime, yellow, gold, orange, red and purple to ochre, ending in dark brown — [Wikipedia, citing Kotopoulou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dallol_(hydrothermal_system))
- White, yellow and brown salt; green, orange and yellow terraced pools; dark hematite domes; yellow native sulfur — [Frontiers 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feart.2019.00351)
